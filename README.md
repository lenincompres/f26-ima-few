# Front-End Web: Explorations in Storytelling
Course Foundation

## Course Description and Purpose
Front-end web development is both a technical practice and an expressive medium. The browser can do more than display information or contain an application: it can reveal, respond, remember, surprise, persuade, and tell stories through structure, image, sound, movement, interaction, data, and participation.
This introductory course teaches HTML, CSS, JavaScript, the DOM, responsive design, data and APIs, browser state, debugging, accessibility, and the iterative development of web projects through explorations of storytelling. Students investigate what stories can become when audiences are invited not only to read or watch, but also to navigate, uncover, manipulate, and influence an experience.
Throughout the semester, these technologies are approached as storytelling materials. Structure can control sequence and revelation; style can create tone, rhythm, and voice; interaction can produce agency, consequence, discovery, and surprise; and data and browser state can become material for documentary work, argument, portraiture, fable, or memory.
Students will experiment with interactive narratives, multimedia essays, playful interfaces, data stories, games, explorable explanations, generative systems, and stranger hybrids. The aim is not to teach one correct form of web storytelling, but to give students the technical foundations, creative confidence, and critical vocabulary to discover forms of their own.
No single definition of storytelling will govern the course. Our shared question is: What can storytelling become in a browser?

## Learning Objectives
By the end of the course, students should be able to:
- create, structure, style, and publish responsive web experiences using HTML, CSS, and JS;
- explain how HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and the DOM work together in the browser;
- use interaction, media, data, and browser state as expressive and narrative materials;
- turn a storytelling intention into choices about form, behavior, accessibility, and participation;
- find and adapt examples, research, debug, and seek help with increasing independence;
- prototype, test, critique, and revise work in response to the experiences of others; and
- document and articulate conceptual and technical discoveries, decisions, and limitations.

## Course Arc
The course follows a technical progression while returning to the expressive possibilities of each layer of the web.
1. **Structure and Meaning:**  HTML, semantic structure, links, media, and web architecture. How can arrangement, navigation, omission, and revelation shape what an audience understands?
2. **Visual Language and Atmosphere:** CSS, typography, composition, layout, responsive design, transitions, and changing styles. How can a page establish voice, mood, emphasis, rhythm, and transformation?
3. **Behavior and Agency:** JavaScript fundamentals, events, objects, classes, and the DOM. What changes when an audience can act, and when the page can respond?
4. **Data, Systems, and Memory:** APIs, JSON, CSV, query strings, cookies, and persistent state. How can outside information, rules, variation, and memory participate in telling a story?
5. **Integration, Testing, and Revision:** Students bring these layers together through increasingly independent projects. Class 14 is devoted to final-project workshop and playtesting; Class 15 to presentation and critique.

## A Course in Exploration
This course explores not only what storytelling can become on the web, but how we learn through discovery. Because technical information is abundant, class time will emphasize shared experimentation, dialogue, problem-solving, and reflection rather than merely reproducing examples or arriving at identical solutions.
At times, I may undertake a problem whose solution I have not predetermined so that we can observe uncertainty, failure, revision, and persistence as parts of creative technical practice.
Some activities and examples may evolve in response to what we discover as a class. I may share what I am trying, noticing, and changing as I examine my own teaching practice in service of your learning. The methods are exploratory; my commitment to your learning is not. Goals, expectations, evaluation, workload, deadlines, accessibility, and support will remain clear.

## Coursework and Assessment
Short experiments build technical fluency while inviting students to test an expressive possibility. Field Notes record what was interesting, confusing, surprising, or newly possible; problems and attempted solutions; connections; unresolved questions; and changes in understanding. Larger projects integrate structure, design, interaction, and concept into coherent web experiences developed through workshop, playtesting, revision, presentation, and critique.
Assessment recognizes technical and conceptual growth, experimentation, reflection, iteration, and care for the audience—not conformity to the instructor’s solution or conventional polish. The course is organized around four equally weighted dimensions of learning:
- Weekly Experiments + Field Notes: 25%
- Midterm Project: 25%
- Shared Learning Process: 25%
- Final Project, Process, Presentation, and Critique: 25%
**Shared Learning Process** recognizes sustained and generous engagement in the learning we create together through classroom experiments, discussion, collaborative problem-solving, workshops, playtesting, critique, and peer support. It does not reward extroversion, constant speaking, technical confidence, or performed enthusiasm. Students may contribute through speaking, making, listening, observing, questioning, testing, documenting, or helping others.
Assignments will include criteria appropriate to their technical and conceptual questions. Assessment may consider experimentation and problem-solving; understanding of the code; coherence among intention, form, interaction, and audience experience; accessibility and attribution; and reflection on discoveries and limitations. Weekly work may use a complete / partial / incomplete scale; larger projects will receive a rubric in advance.

## Course Practices and Logistics

### Schedule
The course meets once a week for three hours over fifteen weeks. The detailed weekly schedule identifies technical and storytelling questions, preparation, assignments, workshops, and major deadlines. Activities and examples may evolve in response to the class, but changes will be communicated clearly and will not unexpectedly increase the workload.

### Tools and Materials
Students need regular access to a laptop with a current browser and code editor. Required software and services are free and include Chrome or Firefox with Developer Tools, an editor such as Visual Studio Code, a GitHub account, and the course repository, wiki, and communication platform; other editors and hosting services are welcome, but no paid tools are required. A limited number of laptops may be available through the department—please speak with me if equipment access becomes a barrier.

### Workload, Deadlines, and Extensions
Assignments are designed to support steady progress through the course rather than last-minute completion. Deadlines help sustain that progression and allow work to contribute to workshops, playtesting, and critique. If circumstances affect your ability to complete work on time, communicate with me as early as possible so we can discuss an extension or alternative plan. Asking for flexibility will not itself lower your grade.

### Attendance
Much of the course depends on experiences that cannot be recreated alone, including shared discovery, collaborative debugging, workshops, playtesting, and critique. If you need to miss a class, please let me know and consult the course materials for what you missed. Repeated absences may affect the Shared Learning Process portion of the grade when they prevent sustained engagement with these experiences, but attendance is not assessed as mere physical presence or used to punish circumstances outside a student’s control.

### Code, Collaboration, Attribution, and AI
Web development builds on documentation, examples, libraries, tutorials, conversation, AI, and other people’s code. Learning from these sources is expected. Credit significant material you borrow or adapt, identify collaborators’ contributions, and disclose substantial AI assistance. Generative AI may be used unless an assignment establishes a different boundary, but you must inspect and verify its output, be prepared to explain and continue developing your work, and remain responsible for its accuracy, accessibility, ethics, and consequences. The [Tisch academic-integrity policies](https://tisch.nyu.edu/student-affairs/important-resources/tisch-policies-and-handbooks) and assignment-specific guidelines apply.

### Technology and Society
Web technologies are not neutral containers. The structures, defaults, datasets, interfaces, and stories we build can include or exclude, reveal or conceal, invite or manipulate, preserve memory or erase context. We will consider not only whether something works, but for whom it works, whose perspective it centers, what assumptions it carries, and what effects it may have. The [ITP/IMA Tech + Society project](https://techandsociety.itp.io/) offers further context for this inquiry.

### Accessibility
We enter the course with different experiences, bodies, languages, responsibilities, and ways of understanding. If any aspect of the course creates an avoidable barrier, I invite you to speak with me so we can consider adjustments while preserving the course's essential goals. Academic accommodations and support are available through the (Moses Center for Student Accessibility)[https://www.nyu.edu/life/safety-health-wellness/students-with-disabilities.html] at 212-998-4980. Students do not need to disclose private medical details to the instructor.

### Community Commitments
This course follows the (ITP/IMA Community Guidelines)[https://community.itp.io/]. Critique should be candid and generous: we will discuss the work rather than diminish the maker, ask questions before imposing interpretations, make space for different forms of participation, and treat confusion as an opportunity for collective inquiry. Consent and care also matter when projects collect information, represent other people, use sensitive material, or ask an audience to participate.
